\begin{abstract}
%he really wants us to quote in text sources and to cite stuff fyi
In particle physics, signal processing is very important in the electronics chain and for the pre-planning phases of an experiment. SPICE is one of the widely used tools used to predict the performance of a circuit[1]. However, by going from detector to digitizer, experimenters can use a full SPICE simulator to get their signal, but doing so requires a detailed schematic of electrical components, which is impractical during the early design phase of an experiment. Our solution to this is the creation of an open source tool that experimenters can use to plan an analog signal to the digitizer path while going through specified components to see how the waveform distorts from the original signal that was produced.
  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In the early planning stages of an experiment, the analog signal path is often considered at a high level and later refined. Experimenters usually start from the detector (photomultiplier tube, drift chamber, or gaseous detectors such as GEMs), and give a brief thought of how the signal will travel from the source to the digitizer. The signal would travel through cables, splitters, amplifiers and discriminators, and be built upon previous experiments without a grasp on possible signal distortion. Using this approach works well for those familiar with the hardware and setups they know well, but fails to anticipate problems such as reflections caused by an impedance mismatch [5] or attenuation due to signal propagation through a cable[6]. 

There are tools that already exist to analyze the path of the analog signal to the digitizer with high precision. Experimenters can use a full SPICE simulator that can accurately find the distortions in the signal from the different components it runs through. But getting this level of accuracy requires a detailed schematic of all the electrical components that are involved, an unrealistic endeavor for the pre-planning phase of an experiment. Experimenters are left between choosing intuition from past experiments or building a complete circuit simulation to get their results.

This project is to fix that gap with an open source tool that is designed specifically for the pre-planning stage of the experiment. This tool is used to let an experimenter describe their signal generator and use it through different components to the digitizer. Depending on what the experimenter chooses, the signal to run through our tool will calculate the predicted outcome of the final result of the signal. This tool will help identify whether reflections occur along with how large they will be, how much the waveform will distort by the time it reaches the digitizer, and how much signal amplitude could be taken or added to the original signal. The goal of this tool is to construct a tool simple and sturdy enough to be useful for experimenters in the planning phase before construction.

\subsection{PMT Signal}
In short, a photomultiplier tube (PMT) is a device used to amplify low levels of light [2]. The signals produced are affected by the specific PMT hardware and the particular light source being used [2]. For our purposes, we focus on signals with characteristic times of approximately 1 to 50 ns; for our research we are focusing on this range. Photons strike at one end of the tube, which holds a photocathode. The photocathode then produces photoelectrons via the photoelectric effect; these are then further amplified by the successive dynodes before being collected by the anode in the form of current caused by the electron avalanche [2]. When the current transits through a load resistor, a signal of voltage as a function of time is extracted. The pulse is fairly predictable: It has a rapid rise before experiencing slow decay. This shape is approximated with a bi-exponential function [3]. There are many forms of the bi-exponential, one being:

$$
f(t) = A \cdot \left[ e^{-\frac{t}{\tau_{\text{fall}}}} - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau_{\text{rise}}}} \right]
$$


Where:
\begin{align*}
f(t) &\quad\to \text{output values at a specific time } (t) \\
A    &\quad\to \text{an arbitrary scaling factor} \\
t    &\quad\to \text{time} \\
\tau_{\text{rise}} &\quad\to \text{Time constant controlling the signal rise time}\\
\tau_{\text{fall}} &\quad\to \text{Time constant controlling the signal fall time}
\end{align*}

%he wsnts units for tau rise and tau fall

The two time constants, ${\tau_{\text{rise}}}$ and ${\tau_{\text{fall}}}$, can be adjusted to match a specific PMT. Real pulses vary in amplitude from one event to the next even under identical conditions [2].

\subsection{Transmission Lines and Impedance matching}

Characteristic impedance is defined as the ratio of voltage to current for a wave traveling through a transmission line. It is given by [4]:

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{R + j\omega L}{G + j\omega C}}$$


A mismatch between the characteristic impedance and the load and source impedance along the circuit pathway will cause a signal to reflect [5]. Signal reflection can be quantified by the reflection coefficient:

$$
\Gamma = \frac{(Z_{\text{load}} - Z_0)}{(Z_{\text{load}} + Z_0)}
$$

Where:
\begin{align*}
\Gamma &\quad\to \text{reflection coefficient} \\
Z_{\text{load}}    &\quad\to \text{impedance of the load} \\
Z_0    &\quad\to \text{characteristic impedance} \\
\end{align*}

 A matched connection ($Z_0$ = $Z_{\text{load}}$) gives no reflection ($\Gamma$ = 0). An open or short end cable yields:

$$
\Gamma_{\text{open}} = \lim_{Z_{\text{load}} \to \infty} \frac{Z_{\text{load}} - Z_0}{Z_{\text{load}} + Z_0} = +1
$$

for an open cable, and: 

$$
\Gamma_{\text{short}} =  \frac{0- Z_0}{0 + Z_0} = -1
$$

for a short cable. A reflection coefficient of 1 indicates that the entire signal was reflected. A reflection coefficient of -1 indicates that the entire signal was reflected and experienced an inversion in its polarity .

Because a signal moves through a transmission line at a finite speed, propagation delay is introduced [6]. Delay is given by:

$$
t_{delay} = \frac{L}{v_f \cdot c}
$$

Where:
\begin{align*}
t_{delay} &\quad\to \text{the signal's shift in seconds} \\
L   &\quad\to \text{length of the cable in meters} \\
v_f    &\quad\to \text{velocity factor (ratio of transmit speed to the speed of light)} \\
c    &\quad\to \text{the speed of light} \\
\end{align*}

Using the time delay, a delayed signal can be expressed as:

$$
V_{delayed}(t) = V_{orginal}(t - t_{delay})
$$

\subsection{Signal Integrity and Decay}
% he wants average speeds for the reader aka how long does it travel in 1 ns

Travel through a cable causes signal attenuation. It is common for cable manufacturers to characterize attenuation in decibels per unit length and as a function of frequency. This convention is seen in data sheets such as the LMR-400 [6]. In order to convert this into a usable scalar we first multiply this quantity by the cable's length [7]:

$$
A_{dB} = \alpha \cdot L
$$

Where:
\begin{align*}
A_{dB} &\quad\to \text{the attenuation experienced by the signal in decibels} \\
\alpha   &\quad\to \text{attenuation per meter in decibels} \\
L   &\quad\to \text{length of the cable in meters} \\
\end{align*}

We can convert this into a usable, unit-less, coefficient through base ten exponentiation: 

$$
A = 10^{-A_{dB}/20}
$$


\subsection{Timing Extraction}

When measuring the properties of a signal, it is often helpful to determine the moment of arrival of said signal. The simplest way of doing this is by utilizing a leading/trailing edge discriminator, which detects the time at which a signal goes beyond or beneath a given threshold. This causes timing walk, as pulses may be recorded as crossing the threshold earlier than smaller pulses despite having arrived at the same time [8]. A leading edge discriminator can be represented mathematically by:  

$$
V(t_{LE}) = V_{th} \quad \text{where} \quad \frac{dV(t_{LE})}{dt} > 0
$$

A trailing edge discriminator can be represented mathematically as: 

$$
V(t_{TR}) = V_{th} \quad \text{where} \quad \frac{  dV(t_{TR})   } {dt} < 0
$$

%wants to know what "v" is and what is the time that we would record

Another detector, the constant fraction discriminator, minimizes the amplitude-based timing errors for signals with similar rise times [8]. Triggering happens when an input signal reaches a fixed percentage of the peak height amplitude. This is often done by adding a delayed copy of the signal to another attenuated copy with reversed polarity :
$$
V_S(t) = V_in(t - t_d) - fV_{in}(t)
$$

the timing point can then be estimated using the zero crossing: 

$$
V_S(t_{CF}) = 0
$$

\section{Methods}
In this project, we present a set  of components that a signal may pass through before reaching a digitizer: splitter, amplifier, edge discriminators, constant fraction discriminators, cables, connectors, and terminators.
When developing this project, we created classes in python for each of the components as well as making a signal generator (PMT Signal), circuit analyzer, and digitizer.

\subsection{Generator}
In our model, we design a signal generator which serves to create synthetic PMT pulses. We pick $A$ to represent the expected number of photo-electrons to be emitted by the photocathode. To do this, we first normalize the unscaled bi-exponential in order to make its integral to infinity equal to one: 

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty} (e^{-\frac{t - t_0}{\tau_{\text{fall}}}} - e^{-\frac{t - t_0}{\tau_{\text{rise}}}} ) u(t- t_0)\,dt = \tau_{\text{fall}} - \tau_{\text{rise}}  
$$

Where $u$ is the step function. Dividing by the integral yields the equation: 

$$
f(t) = \frac{e^{-{t - t_0}/{\tau_{\text{fall}}}} - e^{-{t - t_0}/{\tau_{\text{rise}}}}}{\tau_{\text{fall}} - \tau_{\text{rise}}} u(t-t_0)
$$

When we scale this function by $A$, we get:

$$
\lambda(t) = A\cdot f(t)
$$ 

Where $\lambda(t)$ gives the rate of photo-electron flow at a given time t. At this point, we can define an array of arbitrary length and fill it with zeros in order to store a group of discrete values output from the function:

$$
f = [0,0,0,...]
$$

Additionally, let us store our time values in an array, $t$, of equal size. For a time bin $i$, centered at time $t$.

$$
\mu_i = \lambda(t_i)\Delta t
$$

Poisson statistics are commonly use in low-light PMT measurements. We assume that photoelectrons are produced independently and, as a result, we can sample for a Poisson distribution in order to calculate what the actual number of photoelectrons in that bin may look like [9].   

$$
N_{i} \sim Poisson( \mu_i )
$$

Next, we generate a pulse for every electron in each time bin. We begin by drawing from a gaussian distribution in order to determine the gain of each electron, as not all of them will produce the same pulse. 

$$
G_{ij} = \max(0,\mathcal{N}(1, \sigma^2))
$$

If we draw a negative from the distribution, we clip it to zero. We then model transit time spread using a Gaussian offset. Hamamatsu describes TTS as the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the distribution of transit times [2]. We can convert it to the Gaussian standard deviation through the following: 

$$
\sigma_{TTS} = \frac{  TTS_{FWHM}}   {2\sqrt  {2\ln{2}}  }
$$


$$
\Delta t_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{TTS}^2)
$$

Where $\sigma_{TTS} = \frac{TTS_{FWHM}}{    2\sqrt{2\ln(2)}         }$. After calculating the time delta, we add it to the original start time of the photoelectron. This yields $t_{ij} = t_i + \Delta t_{ij}$, which means that the time of an electron at a given time bin is the time of the time bin summed with the calculated time delta of the electron. 
To calculate the voltage pulse contributed by a single electron we use the following: 

$$
V_{ij} = pA_{SPE}G_{ij}h_{ij}(t)
$$


Where:
\begin{align*}
p &\quad\to \text{Polarity} \\
A_{SPE}   &\quad\to \text{The electron's pulse area} \\
G_{ij}   &\quad\to \text{The random relative gain } \\
h(t)    &\quad\to \text{A normalized bi-exponential with $t_{ij}$ as the pulse starting time and a $\tau_{fall}$ and $\tau_{rise}$ representing the rise and fall time of a single photoelectron pulse } \\
\end{align*}

We sum $V_{ij}$ for every photoelectron in each time bin. the final waveform is: 

$$
\sum_{i} \sum_{j=1}^{N_i} V_{ij}(t)
$$


\subsection{Amplifier}
The amplifier class serves to apply a gain to a passes in voltage array. Gain may either be a dimensionless constant or have units of decibels. If units of decibels are passed in, we use base 10 exponentiation in order to convert it into a usable, unit less constant. 

$$
A = 10^{A_{dB}/20}
$$

Importantly, whatever signal is being passed in must be not be an open circuit voltage signal. %"Can you assert this with some minimun cable?"
This means that it must pass through our cable or connector model first. To begin amplification, we first subtract the voltage signal by the given baseline. %he wants us to explain what a baseline is, dont know exactly where you would want to add it


$$
V_{pulse} = V_{in} - V_{baseline} 
$$

Next, we scale the resulting signal by the given gain.

$$
V_{amplified} = V_{pulse} * A
$$

Scipy's lfilter function is used in order to apply a low and high pass filter. For both the low and high cutoff frequencies, we define the first order parameters to be :  

$$
\tau = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c}
$$

and 
$$
\alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t /\tau}
$$
Where $\Delta t$ is the time between each sample. 

The first order low-pass recurrence is:

$$
y_{LP} = \alpha x[n] + (1 - \alpha)y_{LP}[n-1] 
$$


The high-pass filter used to remove low-frequencies is calculated by subtracting the low-pass result from the input. 


$$
y_{HP} = x[n] - y_{LP}[n]
$$

The recurrence is evaluated using SciPy's lfilter function [10]. In SciPy's coefficient notation:


$$
b = [\alpha]
$$

and 

$$
a = [1, -(1-\alpha)]
$$

The $b$ coefficient multiplies the current input sample. The second coefficient produces the contribution from the previous output. Scipy defined lfilter using the common filter difference equation [10] . 
The initial filter state is: 

$$
z_i = (1 - \alpha) x[0]
$$


This yields:

$$
y[0] = \alpha x[0] + z_i = x[0]
$$

This prevents the beginning of the waveform to have a false starting point, forcing it to instead start at an equivalent voltage value of the original signal. 

Slew rate limits restrict how rapidly the amplifier output can change [11]. If $V_{in}$ is the desired, raw voltage, we can apply the slew rate using the following :

$$
V_{\text{out}}[n] = \text{clip}( V_{\text{in}}[n], V_{\text{out}}[n-1] + S_{\downarrow} \Delta t, V_{\text{out}}[n-1] + S_{\uparrow} \Delta t )
$$

Where:

$$
S_{\uparrow} > 0 \ \text{and} \ S_{\downarrow} < 0 
$$


Here $S_{\uparrow}$ is the maximum gradient that is able to be expressed by the amplifier while the signal is rising, and $S_{\downarrow}$ is the maximum gradient that is able to be expressed by the amplifier while the signal is falling. These effects allow the amplifier to not only change the amplitude, but also the rise and fall times in addition to the signal bandwidth. 



\subsection{Cable}


In our model, we design a cable class that simulates the effects of attenuation and reflections on a signal. When two components are connected with a cable, a voltage divider is formed between the source impedance,$Z_S$, and the characteristic impedance, $Z_0$. The signal launched into the cable is therefore given by: 

$$
V_{launch} = [V_{oc}(t) - V_b]\frac{Z_0}{Z_S+Z_0}
$$


Where $V_oc$ represents the open circuit source voltage. And $V_b$ is the signal baseline

At the boundaries, the respective reflection coefficients are given by:

$$
\Gamma_L = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}
$$

and 

$$
\Gamma_S = \frac{Z_S - Z_0}{Z_S + Z_0}
$$


A reflected signal will likely bounce between the two boundaries more than once. Our model calculates these reflections until the maximum number of round trips is reached, a quantity given by a user defined parameter, or the next arrival is outside of the allotted signal duration. 


The voltage observed at the load can therefore be represented by the sum: 

$$
   V_{c}(t) = V_b + \sum_{n = 0}^{N-1} (1 + \Gamma_L)(\Gamma_L\Gamma_S)^n  V_{p}(t - (2n+1)t_{d})
$$

Where:
\begin{align*}
\Gamma_L &\quad\to \text{Refection coefficient at the load boundary} \\
\Gamma_S  &\quad\to \text{Refection coefficient at the source boundary} \\
t_d  &\quad\to \text{One way propagation delay through the cable} \\
V_p(t)    &\quad\to \text{The initially launched waveform after one way, frequency dependent attenuation} \\
N    &\quad\to \text{The number of arrivals included in the simulation} \\
\end{align*}

For every time the signal goes from the signal to the load and back to the source, we : 

1. The signal is scaled by both the load and source reflection coefficients. After n round trips, this produces: 

$$
(\Gamma_L\Gamma_S)^n
$$

2. Each completed round trip adds a delay equal to twice the one-way delay. Therefore, the nth signal arrival reaches the load after: 

$$
t - (2n+1)t_{d}
$$

3. At the load boundary, the incident and reflected waveforms add [5]. Since: 

$$
V_r = \Gamma_L V_i
$$

The voltage measured at the load is:
$$
V_L = V_i + V_r = V_i + \Gamma_LV_i = (1 + \Gamma_L)V_i
$$

This is why we multiply by $1+\Gamma_L$




\subsection{Connector}
Connectors are junction points. It is a simplified version of the cable that serves to simply connect two components without accounting for attenuation or reflections. As a result, they act as a simple voltage divider.

$$
V_{in}(t) = V_b + [V_S(t) - V_b](\frac{Z_L}{Z_S + Z_L})
$$

\subsection{Discriminators}

A leading/trailing edge discriminator records timing points when the signal crosses a fixed voltage threshold [8]. In our model, we implement both constant fraction discrimination and edge discrimination.

For a pulse of a positive polarity, the leading edge occurs when a signal rises beyond a certain threshold, and the trailing when that signal falls bellow it. These inequalities are reversed when applied to a signal with negative polarity. In order to determine threshold crossing, the model identifies indexes surrounding each crossing, before using linear interpolation to estimate the crossing times. 

The difference between the two gives the time over the threshold. 

$$
t_{TR}-t_{LE}
$$


To build a constant fraction discriminator, we create an attenuated version of the signal and flip it's polarity. 

$$
V_A(t) = - f \cdot V_{in}(t)
$$

We then generate a delayed, non-attenuated version of the signal: 

$$
V_D(t) = V_{in}(t -t_{delay})
$$

Summing these yields : 
$$
V_{S} = V_D  +V_A
$$

The CFD timing point must satisfy the following: 

$$
V_s(T_{CF}) = 0
$$

The model interpolates between the samples surrounding the zero crossing to estimate $T_{CF}$. An arming threshold is used in our model. The CFD will only accept a zero crossing if the pulse has exceeded a given magnitude [8]. After recording a crossing, the model waits for the waveform to return under the arming threshold before accepting another event. this practice prevents small noise fluctuations that appear near the baseline to be mistaken for a true pulse. 



\subsection{Digitizer}
%Can try to make it more spepcific, kinda hard when all it does is just digitizze what the signal looks like
The digitizer models an analog-to-digital converter. Here, it is important to note that the analog waveform is already stored using two arrays, one for time and one for voltage. The Digitizer resamples the wave at a user-selected sampling rate, $f_s$. 

The period if the digitizer is:
$$
T_S = \frac{1}{f_s}
$$

The digitizer sampling times are therefore: 

$$
T_k = t_0 + kT_s
$$
Where k are the integer step indexes, $(0,1,2,3 ...)$.


We then evaluate our waveform at the times in $T_k$ and add a baseline. Because we have limited information on the original wave, as our "analog" representation is discrete, it is highly likely that we do not possess a voltage value for many of the times in $T_k$. As a result, we use linear interpolation in order to estimate voltage values for every given value in $T_k$. 


$$
V(t) = V_{interp}(t_k) + V_{DC}
$$

The offset allows zero volts to correspond to a code above the lowest ADC code. 

Samples outside of the configured input range are clipped: 

$$
V(t)_{clipped} = \operatorname{clip}(V(t), V_{min}, V_{max})
$$

For a digitizer with N bits, the number of outputs code is calculated using: 

$$
M = 2^N
$$

The voltage between two discrete levels, or the least significant bit is represented by: 

$$
V_{LSB} = \frac{V_{max} - V_{min}}{M}
$$

Each clipped voltage is then assigned an integer code: 

$$
D_{c,k}(t) = clip(\lfloor \frac{V_{c,k} - V_{min} }{V_{LSB}}\rfloor, 0, M - 1)
$$

The reconstructed analog voltage $V_k(t)$ is calculated by mapping the digital codes back into the voltage domain using the midpoint reconstruction method:

$$
V_k(t) = V_{min} + (q_k + \frac{1}{2})V_{LSB}
$$

Where $q_k$ is a ADC code in $D_{c,k}$ at time $t$. 


\subsection{Splitter}
The splitter is modeled as a passive three-resistor network. We define $R_1$ as the input resistor, $R_2$ as the output-1 resistor. and $R_3$ as the output-2 resistor. We also define $Z_S$ to be the source impedance, while $Z_{L1}$ and $Z_{L2}$ are the two branch loads.

Define: 

$$
Z_{A} = Z_S + R_1
$$

$$
Z_{B1} = R_2 + Z_{L1}
$$

and 

$$
Z_{B2} = R_3 + Z_{L2}
$$

To find the Thévenin signal at output 1, output 1 is temporarily treated as open while branch 2 remains connected. The output-1 Thévenin voltage is

$$
V_{TH,1}(t) = V_b + [V_{in}(t)-V_b] \frac{Z_{B2}}{Z_A+Z_{B2}}
$$

The equivalent output impedance is

$$
Z_{out,1} = R_2 + (Z_A \parallel Z_{B2})
$$
Similarly, the output-2 Thévenin voltage is

$$
V_{TH,2}(t) = V_b + [V_{in}(t)-V_b] \frac{Z_{B1}}{Z_A+Z_{B1}}
$$
and its output impedance is

$$Z_{out,2} = R_3 + (Z_A \parallel Z_{B1})
$$
When output 1 is connected to its load, its delivered voltage is

$$
V_1(t) = V_b + [V_{TH,1}(t)-V_b] \frac{Z_{L1}}{Z_{out,1}+Z_{L1}}
$$
Output 2 follows the equivalent relationship:

$$
V_2(t) = V_b + [V_{TH,2}(t)-V_b] \frac{Z_{L2}}{Z_{out,2}+Z_{L2}}
$$

This in a ideal splitter model. The model does not account for frequency nor does it introduce propagation delay.

\section{Conclusion}

Planning the analog signal path for particle physics has always been between choosing two paths: experience-based estimations and building a fully detailed simulation. These paths are not suitable for the early planning stages of an experiment. This project was built with the idea to add another path for experimenters to use for their pre-planning stages. It's set to be an open-source tool that has a source, components that could distort a signal, and giving back results about what experimenters want to know (reflections, distortion, and signal loss) before any hardware is built.
